    Senate

    Senate passes bipartisan permitting bill, sending it to the House

    The Senate approved the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act 62–33 late Thursday. It aims to speed federal reviews for energy and building projects. Supporters include the White House and both energy and clean-power industry groups; critics include environmental groups, some tribal organizations and 31 Democrats, plus Republicans Josh Hawley and Mike Rounds. The House is expected to take it up after Thanksgiving.

    Roll Call · CBS News · The Hill · Nov 19, 2026

    Senate

    Senate rejects Iran war powers resolution, 48–51

    The measure would have directed the president to remove U.S. forces from hostilities with Iran. Republicans Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski and Rand Paul voted yes; Democrat John Fetterman voted no. It was the first such vote since the midterms. Opponents noted it would not have gone to the president and so was largely symbolic.

    CBS News · Fox News · Al Jazeera · Nov 19, 2026

    Newest issue · Updated November 18, 2026

    Flights grounded nationwide, and a request for $50 billion from Congress

    • What happened: On Friday, November 13, the FAA stopped all departing flights at every U.S. airport for a little over four hours after a telecommunications outage hit the centers that guide planes. About 3,500 planes were in the air when the stop began. Thousands of flights were canceled or delayed.
    • The cause: Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blamed old, overloaded equipment that caused unstable radar and communications. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was no sign of an attack. On November 15–16, Duffy said contractor L3Harris was replacing old copper wire with fiber-optic cable when a router problem at an FAA facility in Arkansas spread to one in Amarillo, Texas, causing a chain reaction. Two routers are being examined, and Duffy expected flights to be back to full capacity by Monday.
    • The ask to Congress: The administration first asked Congress for $30.1 billion, then raised it to $50 billion late Friday, calling it emergency funding for air traffic control and airports. It wants Congress to act before the end of the year. Congress already approved $12.5 billion for air traffic control upgrades in July 2025. On November 16, President Trump urged Congress to approve the money so the system can be rebuilt quickly rather than in "drips and drabs." On November 17, a letter to lawmakers broke the $50.1 billion into about $10 billion each for air traffic control equipment, airport infrastructure, major hub terminals, five new control centers and up to 140 FAA towers.
    • Why it matters for Congress: This lands during the lame-duck session, when lawmakers are already working on government funding. The new Congress, with Democrats in control of both chambers, starts January 3.

    What the administration says

    • Years of underinvestment by Congress left the system fragile, and more outages could happen without new money.
    • The FAA has already used the 2025 funds to replace copper phone lines and upgrade radios.
    • President Trump and Duffy say the system was left in poor shape by earlier administrations.

    What lawmakers are saying

    • Members of both parties pushed back on the funding request and want to know first what went wrong and whether it could happen again.
    • Critics note the department has spent only part of the money Congress already approved. Duffy says those funds have strict uses and will be spent by 2028.
    • Democrats, including Hakeem Jeffries and Senators Richard Blumenthal and Tammy Duckworth, called for a full investigation. Some also criticized how slowly officials explained the outage to the public.
    • Sen. Brian Schatz and other Democrats proposed paying for upgrades with unspent immigration enforcement money, which Duffy rejected. Speaker Mike Johnson says Congress will take up the request this session, and Transportation Committee Democrats expect an early-December hearing with FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford.

    Sources: NPR · ABC News · Reuters (via Yahoo) · Fox Business · Newsweek · Al Jazeera · AP (via Jamaica Gleaner) · 2026Talks (Kiowa County Press) · Roll Call · AP (via PBS)

    Who can be president?

    • At least 35 years old
    • A natural-born U.S. citizen
    • A U.S. resident for at least 14 years
    • Under the 22nd Amendment, no one can be elected president more than twice. That means Donald Trump can't run again in 2028.

    The steps to the White House

    1. Announce. Candidates often start with an exploratory committee, then formally declare.
    2. Primaries & caucuses. Voters in each state choose delegates, starting early in 2028.
    3. Conventions. Each party's delegates officially pick a nominee and running mate in summer 2028.
    4. General election. Voters cast ballots on November 7, 2028.
    5. Electoral College. 538 electors vote in December, and 270 votes are needed to win. Congress counts the votes on January 6.
    6. Inauguration. The winner is sworn in on January 20, 2029.

      Going to a council meeting for the first time?

      • Meetings are usually open to the public. The agenda is often posted on the town website a few days before.
      • Most councils have a public comment time. You may need to sign up when you arrive or ahead of time, and you'll often get about 2–3 minutes.
      • Say your name and neighborhood, the issue, and what you'd like the council to do.
      • Can't go in person? Many towns stream meetings online or take comments by email.
